# Why Should We Learn Programming in the Age of AI?

I studied Digital Media Art at the China Academy of Art and have used generative AI in my creative practice for several years. I feel fortunate to participate in this change. These tools have made difficult visual ideas more achievable and prompted me to reconsider my role. When AI can already produce programs from natural language, what do I still need from a programming course?

My answer is to understand how a work comes into being. For me, art gains meaning through the artist's experience of life and the choices through which that experience enters a work. Programming can help me turn those choices into rules, examine AI's implementation, and make informed changes when the outcome departs from my intention.

## From Describing to Specifying: Designing for Surprise

I have experienced image tools becoming more capable, from the extra fingers in early Stable Diffusion images and Midjourney's misinterpretations to Nano Banana's precision. Google identifies precise editing and visual consistency as Nano Banana's strengths ([Marini, 2025](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gemini/nano-banana-tips/)). These capabilities are useful, yet I still value the unfamiliarity that early Midjourney sometimes produced. An image could depart from my expectations and open another direction for thought.

This experience makes me want to learn how to create conditions for surprise. In a hypothetical generative poster experiment, “unsettling” describes a feeling. I would still need to decide when repetition breaks, how elements become misaligned, and how frequently anomalies occur. Prompts can also specify constraints; programming helps me trace their execution and change them independently.

For example, I could ask AI to write a program that arranges shapes evenly, then introduces random displacement. Keeping the random seed fixed while varying the displacement would help me compare which change affects the feeling. Precise rules and open-ended results can coexist. I want to learn how to establish these conditions for exploration.

## From Writing to Reading: Judging What to Keep

One successful run tests a program under particular conditions. Suppose repeated runs make the shapes increasingly crowded. Is the density parameter too high, or has the program failed to clear objects from the previous run? The first issue concerns visual choice; the second concerns state management. Understanding variables, loops, and data flow would help me distinguish them and test AI's proposed fix.

AI can explain code, too, but I need to check whether its explanation matches the program's behaviour. Writing small programs myself would give me experience in predicting, running, and revising. Such practice provides a basis for reading critically. A fluent explanation alone offers insufficient evidence.

My art education helps me recognise visual rhythm and mood. Technical understanding makes those judgments easier to implement. Early models' errors sometimes deserve preservation; sometimes they obstruct expression. The work's needs should guide that choice. Syntax may change, while the habits of observing results, locating causes, and recording revisions remain useful.

## Understanding Materials: Seeing Choices Behind the Interface

[Ihde (1990)](https://iupress.org/9780253114167/technology-and-the-lifeworld/) examines technology's involvement in human perception. Applied to digital practice, this suggests that sliders, data formats, and default parameters influence my exploration. Programming offers ways to introduce new relationships: environmental data could determine an image's rhythm, or delayed feedback could shape the viewing experience.

This connects with my interest in the body's representation as data. [Cheney-Lippold (2017)](https://nyupress.org/9781479802449/we-are-data/) examines how systems infer identities from behavioural data. His account makes me wonder how bodies represented through pixels, labels, and engagement metrics influence our understanding of ourselves. One creative direction would let identical inputs produce different “identities” under different classification thresholds. Learning data structures and conditional logic could help me develop this concern into something viewers can manipulate and compare. Basic programming provides only partial insight into commercial models. It can clarify which rules I can inspect and where uncertainty remains.

## Photography and Painting: What Should We Learn After Technological Change?

[Heiferman (2010)](https://siarchives.si.edu/blog/photography-murdered-painting-right) observes that Impressionism, Post-Impressionism, Cubism, and Surrealism developed alongside photography's spread. Painting persisted, and artists gained new possibilities. These movements had complex historical conditions; photography alone cannot explain them. This history nevertheless suggests that relationships between media can be redefined through practice.

Painting's survival cannot guarantee that every programming skill will retain its value. AI has already changed the importance of manually producing code, so I am especially interested in whether this course develops reading, experimentation, and revision. Dylan Beattie's *The Art of Code* includes generative art and live coding, offering a perspective on programming as an expressive medium ([NDC Conferences, 2020](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6avJHaC3C2U)). When rules, time, and behaviour participate in a work, understanding code becomes part of making it.

I hope AI can extend perception: organising time through the slow scale of a growing tree, reconsidering space through a bird's movement, or making wind's paths perceptible. Such ideas require selecting data, establishing mappings, and examining simulation's limitations. They remain human-designed experiments in perspective, unable to replace other beings' experiences.

That is why I am here. I want to extend visual judgment into a work's operation, gaining the ability to propose rules, read implementations, and revise outcomes. As AI undertakes more production, I still need to explain how a work takes shape and which choices carry my ideas.
