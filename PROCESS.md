# AI Use and Writing Process

## Tools and purposes

I used OpenAI Codex to analyse the assignment, research sources, organise the argument, draft and revise the essay in Chinese, translate it into English, and format the references. I supplied my background in Digital Media Art at the China Academy of Art, my experience with Stable Diffusion, Midjourney, and Nano Banana, and my ideas about lived experience, algorithmic classification, and nonhuman perspectives.

AI contributed substantially to both structure and wording. Across several exchanges, I asked it to focus on the assignment's first four suggested directions, preserve my experience, reduce broad art theory, and keep returning to why I should learn programming. I also specified the title and rejected repetitive sentences built around negation followed by a contrasting claim. Codex prepared the English files, checked the word count, and assisted with publishing them to my repository.

## What I kept, and why

I retained the argument that reading AI-generated code requires programming knowledge. It connects my visual judgment with the course: understanding variables, loops, and data flow can help me locate the cause of an unwanted result and evaluate a proposed revision.

The poster program and classification-threshold experiment were proposed by AI to illustrate this argument. They are presented as hypothetical experiments and possible creative directions. I have not claimed to have completed or tested them.

## What I rejected, and why

An early draft used an imagined interactive ink-particle project. I later supplied my own experiences with image-generation tools to make the discussion more personal. Another version copied too much of my notes and made criticism of AI aesthetics the essay's main subject. I objected because the draft drifted away from the assignment's question about learning programming.

The next revision removed too much personal expression. I challenged that version as well: a coherent and relevant essay could still fail to sound like me. The eventual approach connects my experience of unexpected model outputs with learning motivation, my concerns about classification with programmable rules, and my interest in trees, birds, and wind with data and simulation.

I also asked Codex to reduce theoretical name-dropping and repetitive sentence patterns. The essay needed to explain how its judgments followed from experience and reasoning. Familiar philosophical concepts and forceful language could not do that work by themselves.

## Source checking and limits

The online searches and source-page checks in this workflow were primarily carried out by Codex. It consulted a museum article, publisher descriptions and bibliographic records, the speaker's description of his talk, and Google's product information. These checks establish source existence and support limited summaries; they should not be confused with my independently reading the books in full or watching the complete video. The essay uses paraphrases and personal analysis, without direct quotations from these sources.

My own viewing of the assigned talk and reading of the relevant passages must be checked separately from this AI-assisted verification. This record does not claim that those steps have been completed. I remain responsible for checking the sources against my understanding and revising the essay accordingly.

The Chinese drafting conversation took place over multiple revisions. The repository already contained a Schotter programming exercise; its earlier commits document that exercise. The new essay commit records the English version prepared here. Earlier code commits should not be read as evidence of an essay drafting history across several days. Future essay revisions should be committed when they actually occur.
